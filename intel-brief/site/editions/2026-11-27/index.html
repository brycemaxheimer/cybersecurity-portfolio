Juniper Networks Session Smart Conductor · 1 source · first reported 26 November 2026

What happened

SecurityWeek reported on 26 November 2026 that CVE-2026-103406, a critical flaw in Juniper Networks Session Smart Conductor, is being exploited in attacks. According to SecurityWeek, the bug lets a remote attacker run arbitrary commands as root on the device. The vulnerability carries a CVSS score of 9.8, is listed in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, and public proof-of-concept code exists. The sources do not state affected or fixed versions, the attack vector details, or who is behind the exploitation.

Why it matters

Session Smart Conductor centrally manages Juniper Session Smart Routers, so root access on the conductor can give an attacker control over the SD-WAN fabric and a foothold for pivoting into internal Windows and identity infrastructure. Exploitation is confirmed, public PoC code is available and the flaw is in CISA KEV (no due date listed), so the EPSS of 1.5% understates the practical risk. Any conductor with management interfaces reachable from the internet or untrusted networks should be treated as urgent.

What to do

  1. Inventory all Juniper Session Smart Conductor instances and apply the fix from Juniper's advisory for CVE-2026-103406 as soon as possible; the sources do not name fixed versions, so confirm them with Juniper.
  2. Restrict conductor management interfaces so they are not reachable from the internet or untrusted segments until patched.
  3. Review conductor logs, local accounts and configuration for unexpected changes, new users or unknown processes, and treat exposed unpatched conductors as potentially compromised.
  4. Hunt for unusual connections from conductor IP addresses to internal Windows servers, domain controllers and management ports.
  5. Rotate credentials, API keys and certificates stored on or used by any conductor showing signs of compromise.

1Password breach via compromised employee GitHub account exposes customer records and source code

1Password · 1 source · first reported 26 November 2026

What happened

SecurityWeek reported on 26 November 2026 that an attacker used a compromised 1Password employee GitHub account to push malicious code and steal customer records, and that internal source code was also exposed. According to the report, customer vaults were not accessed. The sources do not say which customer data fields were taken, what the malicious code did, or whether it reached any released product build.

Why it matters

Organizations that use 1Password should expect targeted phishing and social engineering that uses the stolen customer records, aimed at password manager admins and users whose credentials guard M365 and Entra ID access. Malicious code was pushed through a developer account, but the scope and any downstream impact on client software are unconfirmed. Treat this as an open supply-chain question until 1Password publishes details.

What to do

  1. Monitor 1Password's official advisories for the exposed data fields, any affected client builds, and required customer actions, and act on them as soon as they are published.
  2. Warn users and 1Password administrators about likely phishing that uses breach themes or stolen account details, and confirm that phishing-resistant MFA is enforced on 1Password admin accounts.
  3. Review 1Password admin and audit logs for unexpected logins, new device approvals, or policy changes since the breach became public.
  4. Inventory 1Password clients and browser extensions in your environment so you can quickly find and update builds if 1Password names a tainted release.
  5. Review your own GitHub organization for weak MFA, stale personal access tokens and unprotected branches, since the same intrusion path applies to your code.

What happened

BleepingComputer reports an ongoing phishing campaign called GoodGuys. It impersonates major brands, including Google, Nvidia, Accenture and SpaceX, and sends fake invitations to paid expert interviews and webinars. The goal is to steal corporate Google and Okta credentials. The available reporting does not describe the delivery infrastructure, the phishing pages, indicators of compromise, or whether MFA is bypassed.

Why it matters

The campaign targets corporate identity providers, so stolen Okta credentials can open every federated SaaS application, which may include Microsoft 365 if Okta federates to Entra ID. BleepingComputer describes the campaign as ongoing. The lure is a paid interview or webinar invitation, a pretext that can get past awareness training built around invoice or password-reset themes.

What to do

  1. Search recent inbound mail for paid expert interview, consultation and webinar invitations that use Google, Nvidia, Accenture or SpaceX branding from non-matching sender domains, and purge confirmed phish.
  2. For any user who clicked or submitted credentials, reset the Okta and Google passwords, revoke active sessions and tokens, and review MFA factors registered recently.
  3. Require phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2/WebAuthn or Okta FastPass) for Okta and Google Workspace sign-ins to blunt credential replay.
  4. Review Okta and Google sign-in logs for new devices, unusual locations or anonymizing infrastructure following suspected lure delivery.
  5. Brief staff that unsolicited paid interview or webinar offers asking them to sign in with corporate credentials are a known phishing pretext.

SYS01 infostealer variant enrolls browsers in management to force a mail-stealing extension

1 source · first reported 26 November 2026

What happened

BleepingComputer reported on 26 November 2026 that a new version of the SYS01 infostealer, spread through Meta advertisements, enrolls victims' browsers into enterprise-style management. According to the report, the malware uses this to force-install a browser extension that steals sensitive data, including Gmail and Outlook email content. The available reporting does not name the affected browsers, the extension, the policy mechanism, or any indicators of compromise.

Why it matters

Abusing browser management policies gives the attacker a persistent, policy-enforced extension that users cannot easily remove and that can read webmail sessions, including Outlook on the web used with Microsoft 365. Unexpected browser policy changes on Windows endpoints are a durable signal, because legitimate extension force-install and cloud enrollment settings normally come only from Group Policy, Intune or other management tooling. The only source is a short news summary, so the exact technique should be confirmed once fuller details are published.

What to do

  1. Hunt for browser policy registry writes (ExtensionInstallForcelist, cloud management enrollment token values) under the Chrome, Edge and Brave policy keys made by processes other than your Group Policy, Intune or configuration management agents.
  2. Audit force-installed and policy-managed extensions across Chrome and Edge, and remove any that are not on your approved list.
  3. Enforce an extension allowlist through your own Group Policy or Intune so that unknown extensions are blocked by default.
  4. If a host is affected, remove the rogue policy keys and extension, then revoke sessions and reset passwords for the user's Microsoft 365 and Gmail accounts.
  5. Warn users about software downloads promoted through Facebook and Instagram ads, the delivery channel reported for SYS01.

Explainer

Background, not news

Browser policy abuse: force-installed extensions as an inbox reader

Attackers who can write browser management policy, or enroll a browser into management they control, can silently install extensions that read webmail and other pages inside the user's already-authenticated session.

Why today. The SYS01 infostealer now enrolls victims' browsers into enterprise-style management to force-install a mail-stealing extension, and Remus reads Gmail through a fake Google Sheets extension that its operators sold as a Gmail zero-day. Both work through the browser rather than through any flaw in Gmail or Outlook. See story 7, story 6.

Why an extension can read your mail

A browser extension declares host permissions in its manifest, for example access to a webmail domain. If the user or policy grants them, its content scripts run inside those pages and can read and change the rendered content, including open messages, folder lists and search results. Some permissions also let an extension read cookies or watch requests.

None of this needs the user's password, an OAuth token or a server-side bug. The extension acts inside a session the user already signed in to, so MFA, conditional access at sign-in and phishing-resistant authenticators have already been satisfied. That is why Remus can describe a working Gmail capability as a zero-day when no Gmail vulnerability is involved.

Enterprise policy as the install mechanism

Chromium-based browsers, including Chrome and Edge, read enterprise policy. On Windows this comes from registry keys under the Policies branch for each vendor, written by Group Policy, Intune or anything else with write access. Chrome can also enroll into Chrome Browser Cloud Management with an enrollment token, after which a remote admin console pushes policy. Firefox has its own policy mechanism.

The ExtensionInstallForcelist policy is the key one for attackers. Extensions listed there install silently, cannot be removed or disabled by the user, and are re-installed if deleted. A browser that is managed shows a notice such as 'Managed by your organization', but users seldom treat that as a warning. The SYS01 reporting does not say which browser or policy path the malware uses, so assume any of these routes is possible.

What it looks like in telemetry

On endpoints, the strongest signal is a write to browser policy registry keys, especially extension force-install lists or a cloud enrollment token, by a process that is not your management agent, Group Policy client or a known installer. In Defender XDR, DeviceRegistryEvents can be filtered on the Chrome and Edge policy key paths and the initiating process. New extension folders under browser profile directories appear in DeviceFileEvents.

On a live machine, the browser's internal policy page lists every active policy and its source, and the management page shows whether and by whom the browser is managed. Defender Vulnerability Management can inventory installed browser extensions and their permissions, which helps you spot an unknown extension with mail-domain access across the fleet.

Prevention

Manage the browsers yourself. An extension allowlist, with a blocklist of all extensions except those approved, sets a policy baseline that attacker-written policy has to visibly override. Restricting which extensions may run on sensitive sites such as webmail and SSO portals narrows the damage further.

Because these attacks rely on writing policy, the main control is keeping malware from getting a foothold at all. Typical delivery is through malicious ads and fake software downloads, as with SYS01's Meta ads. Users should not have local admin rights, because writes to the machine-wide policy keys require them.

For defenders

  1. Hunt in DeviceRegistryEvents for writes to Chrome and Edge policy keys, in particular extension force-install lists and cloud management enrollment tokens, where the initiating process is not your Intune, Group Policy or software deployment tooling.
  2. Deploy an extension allowlist for Chrome and Edge through Intune or Group Policy, with all other extensions blocked, so any unexpected force-install entry stands out against your baseline.
  3. Use the Defender Vulnerability Management browser extension inventory to find extensions with access to Gmail, Outlook on the web or your SSO domains, and review any you did not approve.
  4. On a suspected infostealer host, check the browser policy and management pages and the installed extension list before reimaging, and remove attacker-written policy as well as the malware itself.
  5. After cleanup, revoke the affected user's sessions and refresh tokens in Entra ID and Google, because an extension running inside the session may also have exposed session material.
