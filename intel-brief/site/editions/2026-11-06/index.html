What happened

watchTowr Labs published a technical analysis on 5 November 2026 of two Barracuda flaws: CVE-2026-116606, a pre-authentication authorization bypass in Email Security Gateway (CVSS 9.3), and CVE-2026-111560, an OS command injection (CVSS 8.6) that also affects several other Barracuda appliances. According to watchTowr, chaining the two gives an unauthenticated attacker remote root code execution on the ESG. watchTowr compared vulnerable ESG 9.4.0.027 with patched 9.4.0.029 and says Barracuda shipped several patch files for the issues. Public proof-of-concept material is reported for CVE-2026-116606; the available source does not itself document in-the-wild exploitation of these two CVEs, although the story is tagged as exploited.

Why it matters

ESG sits at the mail edge and inspects all inbound mail, and its predecessor flaw CVE-2023-2868 was exploited as a zero-day by UNC4841, according to Mandiant via watchTowr, forcing physical appliance replacement. Neither new CVE is in CISA KEV yet and EPSS is low (0.7% and 0.5%), but a public technical write-up plus PoC for a pre-auth root chain on an internet-facing appliance usually shortens time to mass exploitation. Treat any unpatched ESG as exposed now.

What to do

  1. Confirm every Barracuda ESG is on 9.4.0.029 or later and that Barracuda's patch files for both CVEs are applied; patch other affected Barracuda products per Barracuda's advisory.
  2. Restrict the ESG administrative web interface so it is not reachable from the internet.
  3. Review ESG logs and outbound traffic from the appliance for unexpected connections, new processes or configuration changes since early November 2026.
  4. Hunt for authentication attempts and lateral connections originating from the ESG appliance IP toward internal hosts.
  5. If compromise is suspected, isolate the appliance, rotate credentials stored on or used by it (LDAP bind, SMTP relay, admin accounts) and engage Barracuda support.
Affected
  • Barracuda Email Security Gateway (CVE-2026-116606, CVE-2026-111560); watchTowr tested 9.4.0.027 as vulnerable
  • CVE-2026-111560 also affects Barracuda Load Balancer ADC, Message Archiver, WAF Control Center, Web Application Firewall and Web Security Gateway (versions not stated)
Fixed
  • Barracuda ESG 9.4.0.029

What happened

On 5 November 2026, SANS Internet Storm Center reported honeypot traffic that it believes targets CVE-2023-20198, the critical authentication bypass in the Cisco IOS XE web UI. The requests post SOAP-formatted command-execution messages to the web services management endpoint. They URL-encode one character of the path, which SANS suggests may be meant to slip past simplistic WAF signatures. The traffic came from the exposure-management platform RootEvidence, which sent nonstandard opt-out headers that SANS had not seen before, and SANS describes the activity as nothing new.

Why it matters

CVE-2023-20198 has a CVSS score of 10.0 and is listed in CISA KEV with confirmed in-the-wild exploitation. Its EPSS score is 99.6%, and public exploit code exists. Any IOS XE switch or router, or affected Rockwell Stratix device, that still exposes the HTTP/HTTPS web UI to untrusted networks is being continuously probed and should be treated as at risk of full device takeover.

What to do

  1. Identify every IOS XE and Stratix device with the HTTP/HTTPS server enabled and confirm it runs a release Cisco lists as fixed for CVE-2023-20198.
  2. Disable the web UI (ip http server / ip http secure-server) on internet-facing devices, or restrict it to trusted management networks via ACLs, until patched.
  3. Check exposed devices for compromise: unexpected local accounts with high privilege, unknown configuration changes, and signs of the implant described in Cisco's original advisory.
  4. Review web/WAF and firewall logs for requests to the web services management endpoint, including URL-encoded variants of the path.
  5. Make sure WAF and IDS rules normalize URL encoding before matching, so encoded path variants are not missed.
Affected
  • Cisco IOS XE with the web UI (HTTP/HTTPS server) feature enabled; specific version ranges not stated in the sources
  • Rockwell Automation Allen-Bradley Stratix 5200 and 5800 firmware (per NVD product data; versions not stated)

Fake OpenAI, SpaceX and Anthropic event invitations used to steal Microsoft and Google accounts

Microsoft Microsoft 365 · 1 source · first reported 5 November 2026

What happened

BleepingComputer reports that attackers are sending employees fake event invitations that impersonate OpenAI, SpaceX and Anthropic. Victims are lured into phishing pages that steal Microsoft and Google account credentials and authentication sessions. According to the report, the campaign runs on two different phishing-as-a-service platforms. The available excerpt does not name the platforms, sender infrastructure or indicators.

Why it matters

The sources say session tokens are stolen as well as passwords. That points to adversary-in-the-middle style phishing, which can bypass standard MFA and give attackers direct access to Microsoft 365 and Entra ID. Themed AI and space-industry event lures are timely and believable to many staff, and phishing-as-a-service kits let many operators reuse the same tooling at scale.

What to do

  1. Search mail for invitations themed on OpenAI, SpaceX or Anthropic events. Purge delivered messages and identify any users who clicked.
  2. For users who interacted with these lures, revoke Entra ID sessions and refresh tokens, reset passwords, and review MFA methods registered after the click.
  3. Enforce phishing-resistant MFA (FIDO2 or Windows Hello for Business) and Conditional Access requiring compliant or hybrid-joined devices, to limit the value of stolen sessions.
  4. Review compromised accounts for persistence such as new inbox rules, mail forwarding, OAuth app consents and new MFA devices.
  5. Warn staff that unsolicited AI or tech-company event invitations asking them to sign in are a current phishing theme.

Microsoft: threat actors use agentic AI framework to harvest GitHub secrets and pivot into Azure

1 source · first reported 5 November 2026

What happened

Microsoft Threat Intelligence reports that threat actors are using an agentic AI offensive framework, named ARTEX, to scan public GitHub repositories for exposed credentials, application secrets and AI inference API keys, then use AI agents to analyze results and carry out follow-on attacks. In one case Microsoft says an agent went from scanning GitHub to exfiltrating data from Azure Key Vault in five minutes. Microsoft also says that from 28 October 2026 it saw successful service principal sign-ins from 199.19.77.84 across multiple application IDs, including a compromised multitenant vendor application credential used to reach downstream tenants. The activity included resource discovery, credential and data theft from Key Vaults and Storage Accounts, and abuse of Azure AI services. Microsoft also flagged 65.87.223.26 for exploitation attempts via shell commands in user agents and attempts to obtain App Service authentication tokens. The published article is truncated, so some campaign details are missing.

Why it matters

Microsoft confirms exploitation in the wild. Any client secret, certificate or API key committed to a public repository, including one held by a third-party vendor whose multitenant app you have consented to, can lead to service principal access to Key Vaults, storage and AI resources in your Entra tenant within minutes. Service principal sign-ins are often monitored less closely than user sign-ins, so this activity can go unnoticed without a dedicated hunt.

What to do

  1. Hunt for service principal sign-ins and cloud activity from 199.19.77.84 and 65.87.223.26 since 28 October 2026. If you find any, rotate the credentials of the affected applications and revoke their sessions.
  2. Scan your organization's public and internal GitHub repositories for committed secrets, enable secret scanning and push protection, and rotate any secret that has ever been exposed.
  3. Inventory multitenant third-party applications with consent in your tenant and review their permissions and recent service principal sign-in activity. Remove apps that are unused or overprivileged.
  4. Restrict Key Vault and Storage Account access with private endpoints or network rules, apply least-privilege RBAC, and alert on bulk secret retrieval by service principals.
  5. Prefer managed identities or workload identity federation over long-lived client secrets, and apply Conditional Access for workload identities where licensed.

FBI, CISA and partners warn of active attacks on industrial refrigeration and cold-chain controllers

2 sources · first reported 5 November 2026

What happened

On 5 November 2026, CISA, the FBI, HHS, USDA and the Department of Transportation published a joint advisory on malicious cyber activity against industrial refrigeration and cold-chain monitoring systems worldwide. The agencies say actors are targeting electronic temperature controllers and commercial refrigeration controllers in retail grocery and cold storage facilities. They exploit authentication bypass, code injection and weak configurations on unpatched or internet-reachable devices to change settings, including forcing units into continuous heating and defrost cycles. BleepingComputer reports possible food spoilage, equipment damage and fire hazards; the advisory does not name specific vendors, products or CVEs.

Why it matters

These controllers are often installed by facilities or refrigeration contractors and connected to corporate or guest networks, or exposed directly to the internet, without the security team knowing. The agencies describe the activity as ongoing. Impacts include inventory loss, safety hazards and loss of temperature visibility, which matter most to healthcare, food, chemical and transport organizations. Because no products or CVEs are named, defenders have to start from asset discovery and exposure reduction rather than patching.

What to do

  1. Identify all refrigeration, temperature-controller and cold-chain monitoring devices, including those managed by third-party contractors, and confirm none are reachable from the public internet.
  2. Remove direct internet exposure of controller panels and web interfaces. Put any required remote access behind a VPN or secure gateway with MFA.
  3. Change default or shared passwords on controllers and monitoring platforms, and enforce strong unique credentials.
  4. Apply vendor firmware and software updates to controllers and monitoring systems, and ask vendors which versions address authentication bypass and code injection issues.
  5. Segment refrigeration and building-management devices from IT networks, and alert on unexpected setpoint, defrost-cycle or alarm changes.

Microsoft Microsoft Teams · 1 source · first reported 5 November 2026

What happened

Microsoft Threat Intelligence says it detected roughly 7.3 billion email phishing threats from July through September 2026, with September alone reaching nearly 3 billion, a 41% rise over August. Microsoft reports that QR code phishing stayed well below its early-year peak, while CAPTCHA-gated phishing grew again, partly through SVG attachments. It also counted about 129 million malicious messages spoofing tracked business service domains, many of which were delivered because organization or user exceptions overrode protections after the messages failed authentication. Microsoft highlighted an August campaign against legal organizations that used salary review documents and QR codes to lead to device code phishing, a September campaign that used purchase order and payroll lures with disguised archives to deliver Agent Tesla, and Teams social engineering that increasingly uses ordinary personal names rather than help desk personas.

Why it matters

This report covers no vulnerability, but it points to a configuration weakness many M365 tenants have: broad allow rules and safe-sender exceptions that deliver mail Defender has already flagged as malicious. Device code phishing, SVG-borne CAPTCHA lures, archive-delivered infostealers and Teams impersonation all lead to credential theft. Explainer

Background, not news

Service principals and multitenant app credentials in Entra ID

A service principal is the identity an application uses to sign in to a Microsoft Entra ID tenant, and anyone holding its secret or certificate can act as that application with whatever permissions it was granted.

Why today. Microsoft reports attackers harvesting application secrets from public GitHub repositories, signing in as service principals across multiple app IDs, and using a compromised multitenant vendor application credential to reach downstream customer tenants and their Key Vaults and Storage Accounts. See story 4.

Application objects, service principals and credentials

In Entra ID an app registration creates an application object in its home tenant. Every tenant that uses the app, including the home tenant, gets a service principal, which appears as an enterprise application. Permissions, role assignments and Azure RBAC grants attach to the service principal in each tenant.

The app proves its identity with a credential: a client secret, a certificate, or a federated credential that trusts tokens from an outside issuer such as GitHub Actions. Client secrets are plain strings, which is why they end up in source code, configuration files and CI pipelines. A secret leaked to a public repository can be used from anywhere, with no device, user or MFA involved.

Why a multitenant app credential reaches many tenants

For a multitenant app, credentials live on the application object in the vendor's home tenant. Each customer that consented to the app has its own service principal, but that principal accepts tokens from the same app credential. An attacker who steals the vendor's secret or certificate can request tokens for any customer tenant where the app is consented and receive whatever permissions that customer granted.

This is why application permissions matter so much. Permissions such as reading all mail or files, or Azure roles over Key Vaults and Storage Accounts, are exercised without a signed-in user. Activity therefore appears as the application, not as any person in the customer tenant.

Why user-focused controls miss it

Service principal sign-ins do not go through MFA, and Conditional Access policies built for users do not apply to them. Conditional Access for workload identities can restrict sign-ins by location or risk, but it requires a Workload Identities Premium license. It also applies only to single-tenant service principals registered in your own tenant. Microsoft documents third-party multitenant and SaaS apps as out of scope, so a stolen vendor credential is largely governed by what you consented to and what you monitor.

What it looks like in telemetry

Service principal sign-ins are logged separately from user sign-ins. In Sentinel they arrive in the AADServicePrincipalSignInLogs table when that diagnostic category is enabled. In Defender XDR advanced hunting they appear in the AADSpnSignInEventsBeta table. Hunt for sign-ins from IP addresses or ASNs an app has never used, or one IP authenticating as several different app IDs, which matches the pattern Microsoft describes.

For follow-on activity, look at Entra audit logs for credentials added to applications or service principals and new role or permission grants. In Key Vault diagnostic logs, look for secret, key or certificate reads by unfamiliar identities or from new IPs. Storage account logs show bulk listing or downloads. Microsoft Entra ID Protection can flag leaked credentials and suspicious sign-ins for workload identities, subject to licensing.

Reducing the risk

For your own workloads, prefer managed identities or workload identity federation, which leave no long-lived secret to leak. Where a credential is unavoidable, prefer certificates over client secrets, keep lifetimes short and store them in a vault rather than in code. Enable secret scanning and push protection on repositories.

For third-party apps, the controls are consent and scope. Restrict user consent, require admin review, and periodically audit which multitenant apps hold application permissions or Azure roles in your tenant. Remove grants that are no longer needed, and limit Key Vault and storage access with least-privilege RBAC and network restrictions so a stolen app identity reaches as little as possible.

For defenders

  1. Hunt AADServicePrincipalSignInLogs or AADSpnSignInEventsBeta for sign-ins from 199.19.77.84 and for any single IP authenticating as multiple application IDs since 28 October 2026.
  2. Inventory multitenant enterprise applications that hold application permissions or Azure RBAC roles in your tenant, and remove grants that are unused or broader than needed.
  3. Review Entra audit logs for recently added secrets or certificates on applications and service principals, and rotate any credential found in a public or internal repository.
  4. Check Key Vault and Storage Account diagnostic logs for secret reads and bulk data access by service principals from new IP addresses, and confirm those logs are actually enabled and sent to Sentinel.
  5. Move your own workloads from client secrets to managed identities or workload identity federation, and enable secret scanning with push protection on code repositories.
