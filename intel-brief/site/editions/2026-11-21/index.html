Also: CVE-2026-123890 (KEV, due 23 Nov), CVE-2026-76466 (KEV, due 23 Nov), CVE-2025-42884 (KEV, due 23 Nov)

What happened

On 20 November 2026 CISA added four vulnerabilities to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, citing evidence of active exploitation, with a federal remediation due date of 23 November 2026. CISA describes them as an unauthenticated code execution flaw through deserialization in the Apache Roller XML-RPC endpoint (CVE-2026-129448), an authentication bypass in the VMware Directory Service of Broadcom VMware vCenter (CVE-2026-123890), a hard-coded credentials flaw in clustered Cisco Expressway Series devices that allows root command execution through the database service (CVE-2026-76466), and a JNDI injection flaw in SAP NetWeaver Enterprise Portal that lets an unauthenticated attacker disclose or modify server information (CVE-2025-42884). CISA notes that the Roller CVE covers the same underlying bug as CVE-2026-82384 but applies to a wider set of versions. The sources give no affected or fixed version numbers and do not name the attackers.

Why it matters

vCenter, Expressway, NetWeaver portals and Roller blogs are often reachable from the internet. A compromise gives an attacker control of virtualization infrastructure, collaboration edge gateways or application servers. Three of the flaws are rated CVSS 9.8 and have public proof-of-concept code. EPSS ranges from 0.7% for the SAP flaw to 5.5% for Roller, but exploitation is already confirmed and the KEV due date of 23 November 2026 leaves only three days.

What to do

  1. Apply the vendor fixes or mitigations from Broadcom, Cisco, SAP and Apache for all four CVEs before 23 November 2026, starting with internet-exposed vCenter, Expressway and Roller instances.
  2. Remove internet exposure of vCenter management interfaces and the Expressway database and cluster services, and restrict them to management networks.
  3. Disable or block the Apache Roller XML-RPC endpoint if it is not needed until patched.
  4. Do compromise triage on systems that were exposed before patching. Look for new vCenter SSO accounts and logins, unexpected root activity on Expressway, and shells spawned by Java on Roller and NetWeaver hosts.
  5. Rotate credentials stored on or used by vCenter and Expressway if you find signs of compromise.

  • Cisco Expressway Series, clustered devices (CVE-2026-76466; versions not stated)
  • SAP NetWeaver Enterprise Portal (CVE-2025-42884; versions not stated)

N-able Mail Assure, SpamExperts Hosted Cloud · 2 sources · first reported 20 November 2026

What happened

N-able says attackers breached one of its servers and caused a major worldwide outage of its Mail Assure and SpamExperts Hosted Cloud email filtering services, according to BleepingComputer. SecurityWeek reports that N-able blamed the disruption on a cyberattack and that the incident could have led to credential theft. The sources do not say how the attackers got in, which data or credentials were exposed, or whether service has been fully restored.

Why it matters

Organizations that route Microsoft 365 or other mail through Mail Assure or SpamExperts may have lost inbound filtering or mail flow, and any credentials stored in or used with those services may be exposed. Because the email gateway sits in the mail path, a compromise there could enable mail interception or abuse of connector and relay settings. The sources give no indicators, so the immediate work is confirming exposure and rotating secrets.

What to do

  1. Confirm whether your organization or your MSP uses Mail Assure or SpamExperts Hosted Cloud and follow N-able's incident communications for scope and restoration status.
  2. Rotate any credentials, API keys, SMTP relay or connector secrets, and admin passwords associated with Mail Assure or SpamExperts accounts, and enforce MFA on those admin portals.
  3. Review Exchange Online inbound and outbound connectors and transport rules tied to the filtering service for unexpected changes, and plan temporary direct mail flow or alternate filtering if the outage persists.
  4. While upstream filtering is degraded, raise scrutiny on inbound mail: confirm Defender for Office 365 policies are active and watch for phishing that slips through.
  5. Hunt for unusual sign-ins and mailbox rule creation for accounts whose credentials may have passed through the affected services.

What happened

Unit 42 published an analysis on 20 November 2026 of campaigns by affiliates of the Com, a loose cybercriminal community, that use voice phishing to gain access, steal data from cloud environments and then extort victims. According to Unit 42, the activity has targeted healthcare and finance organizations, and the report includes defense guidance. The available summary does not list specific techniques, tools, indicators or affected platforms.

Why it matters

Vishing campaigns of this kind typically go after help desks and individual users to reset credentials or MFA, which turns into identity compromise in Entra ID and bulk data theft from SaaS apps without any malware on endpoints. Unit 42 describes the activity as active, and healthcare and finance teams are named targets. Because no specific tradecraft is in the summary, defenders should read the full report and treat the guidance here as general hardening and hunting.

What to do

  1. Require strong caller verification at the help desk before any password reset, MFA reset or new MFA device enrollment, such as a callback to a number on file or manager approval.
  2. Enforce phishing-resistant MFA for privileged and high-value accounts, and limit who can register new authentication methods through Conditional Access.
  3. Alert on new MFA method registration or password reset followed quickly by a sign-in from an unfamiliar location or device.
  4. Monitor SaaS and cloud storage for unusually large download or export volumes per user, and review OAuth app consents.
  5. Review the full Unit 42 report for indicators and specific techniques and add them to detections.

What happened

Rapid7 says its latest Metasploit release adds a shared Zimbra SOAP mixin and two auxiliary modules from its Zimbra identity-boundary research. CVE-2026-73571 is an authorization bypass in SaveDraftRequest handling that lets any authenticated user send mail as another local mailbox. CVE-2026-93637 is a rate-limit bypass in the Network Edition GraphQL authentication endpoint that lets credential stuffing evade the source-IP lockout applied to SOAP logins. Both were validated against Zimbra 10.1.16 Network Edition. The same release adds an exploit module for CVE-2026-102764, which the sources tie to BeyondTrust Remote Support, not Zimbra, despite how the release is framed.

Why it matters

The two Zimbra flaws are rated low (CVSS 3.1 and 3.7, EPSS 0.9% and 1.0%) and are not in KEV, but public Metasploit modules make internal mail spoofing and lockout-free password spraying easy. That raises phishing and account-takeover risk for organizations running Zimbra. The bigger concern is CVE-2026-102764 in BeyondTrust Remote Support: CVSS 9.3, EPSS 55.6%, listed in CISA KEV (no due date given), and now available as a ready-made exploit, so internet-facing Remote Support appliances should be treated as urgent.

What to do

  1. Inventory internet-facing BeyondTrust Remote Support appliances and apply the vendor fix for CVE-2026-102764 immediately, since it is in KEV and a public exploit module exists.
  2. Check whether you run Zimbra Network Edition 10.1.16 or nearby releases and apply Synacor's fixes for CVE-2026-73571 and CVE-2026-93637 once you confirm the patched versions with the vendor.
  3. Restrict or monitor access to the Zimbra GraphQL authentication endpoint and enforce MFA so password spraying that bypasses lockout cannot succeed.
  4. Review Zimbra mail logs for messages sent as one mailbox by a different authenticated account without a delegation grant, and for unexpected items in users' Sent folders.
  5. Review BeyondTrust Remote Support logs and hosts for unexpected file uploads and for outbound activity originating from the appliance.

Explainer

Background, not news

JNDI injection and Java deserialization: when a server builds objects from attacker input

JNDI injection and unsafe deserialization are related Java flaws where an application lets attacker-supplied data decide which object it creates or which remote server it fetches one from, often leading to code execution on the server.

Why today. Two of the four flaws CISA added to KEV on 20 November 2026 belong to this family: a JNDI injection flaw in SAP NetWeaver Enterprise Portal and an unauthenticated deserialization flaw in the Apache Roller XML-RPC endpoint. Federal agencies have only until 23 November 2026 to remediate. See story 1.

How deserialization goes wrong

Serialization turns an in-memory object into bytes so it can be stored or sent, and deserialization rebuilds the object from those bytes. In Java, native deserialization can create objects of any class on the application's classpath, and some of those classes run code automatically while being rebuilt. If an application deserializes data that an unauthenticated user controls, the attacker chooses which classes are created and with what values.

Attackers chain together ordinary library classes, called gadgets, so that rebuilding the object triggers a sequence of method calls ending in something dangerous, such as running a command. The application's own code does not need a bug beyond accepting untrusted serialized data. Whether a gadget chain exists depends on which libraries ship with the product, so exploitability varies by product and version. The same pattern appears in XML-based and other formats that map input onto arbitrary types, not only Java's native binary format.

How JNDI injection works

JNDI is Java's standard interface for looking up named resources, such as a database connection or a directory entry, and it can resolve names against LDAP, RMI, DNS and other providers. JNDI injection happens when an application passes an attacker-controlled string into a lookup. The attacker supplies a name pointing at a server they run, and the vulnerable application connects out to it.

The attacker's server can answer with a reference telling the client to load a factory class from a remote location, or with a serialized object that the client then deserializes. Newer Java runtimes disable loading remote class code by default, but that does not close the hole: attackers can still return references to factory classes already present on the server, or serialized gadget chains. This is the mechanism behind Log4Shell. CISA's description of the SAP flaw speaks of disclosing or modifying server information rather than code execution, so treat the impact as product-specific and follow SAP's own advisory.

What it looks like in telemetry

The clearest host signal is a Java process starting a shell or scripting engine. On Windows that means java.exe or javaw.exe as the parent of cmd.exe, powershell.exe, rundll32.exe or certutil.exe, which you can hunt for in the DeviceProcessEvents table in Defender XDR. On Linux appliances, look for java spawning sh, bash, curl or wget.

JNDI injection has a network signal of its own: an application server making outbound LDAP, RMI or HTTP connections to an unfamiliar internet host just after receiving a request. Look for these in DeviceNetworkEvents, firewall logs in Sentinel, or proxy logs. Web logs may show long serialized or encoded blobs in POST bodies to endpoints such as XML-RPC, or lookup-style strings in parameters and headers, though attackers routinely obfuscate them. After exploitation, expect new files in web roots, new scheduled tasks or services, and credential access from the service account.

How to prevent it

Patching is the real fix, because the flaw lives in how the product handles input. Where a vulnerable endpoint is not needed, such as an XML-RPC interface, disable it or block it at the reverse proxy. Strict egress filtering on application servers blunts JNDI injection, because the attack depends on the server reaching an attacker-controlled host.

For in-house Java code, avoid native deserialization of untrusted data. Where it cannot be avoided, apply Java serialization filters to allow only expected classes, and never pass user input to JNDI lookups. WAF signatures help against commodity scanning but are easy to evade, so do not count them as mitigation.

For defenders

  1. Inventory internet-facing SAP NetWeaver Enterprise Portal and Apache Roller instances and patch them against the newly listed KEV flaws now. The federal due date is 23 November 2026, only three days after listing.
  2. If Roller's XML-RPC endpoint is not used, disable it or block it at the reverse proxy until patched.
  3. Hunt in DeviceProcessEvents for java.exe or javaw.exe spawning cmd.exe, powershell.exe or other LOLBins on application servers. Baseline legitimate cases and alert on the rest.
  4. Restrict outbound traffic from application servers to required destinations only. Alert on outbound LDAP, RMI or unexpected HTTP from those hosts to the internet.
  5. Treat any server that was exposed and unpatched as possibly compromised. Check web roots for new files, look for new services or scheduled tasks, and rotate credentials held by the application's service account.
