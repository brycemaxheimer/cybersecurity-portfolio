Microsoft reports Entra Connect Health flaw exploited to expose admin emails and tenant configuration

Microsoft Entra Connect Health · 1 source · first reported 21 November 2026

What happened

BleepingComputer reports that Microsoft is warning organizations of a data breach in which attackers exploited a vulnerability in the Entra Connect Health service. According to that report, the attackers gained unauthorized access to tenant administrator email addresses and tenant configuration information. No CVE, affected component details, timeline or count of affected tenants were given in the available reporting, and the source text is a truncated summary.

Why it matters

The flaw sits in a Microsoft-operated cloud service, so the fix is on Microsoft's side, but leaked admin addresses and configuration data give attackers a ready target list for phishing, password spraying and MFA fatigue against privileged Entra accounts. Exploitation is reported as confirmed, so treat any tenant using Entra Connect Health as potentially exposed until Microsoft's notification says otherwise.

What to do

  1. Check the Microsoft 365 admin center Message center and Service health for a breach notification specific to your tenant, and follow any instructions it contains.
  2. Confirm all Global Administrator and other privileged Entra accounts use phishing-resistant MFA and are covered by Conditional Access policies.
  3. Warn administrators that their addresses may be exposed and raise scrutiny of phishing and unexpected MFA prompts aimed at them.
  4. Review Entra audit logs for unexpected changes to Connect Health agents, service principals, app consents and tenant settings since the disclosure.
  5. Monitor sign-in logs for password spraying or anomalous sign-ins against admin accounts.

Rackspace Spot breach exposed Kubernetes secrets in customer clusters via compromised credentials

Rackspace Rackspace Spot · 1 source · first reported 21 November 2026

What happened

According to BleepingComputer, Rackspace has warned customers that attackers broke into its Rackspace Spot cloud platform using compromised credentials. The intruders were able to deploy unauthorized workloads and read Kubernetes secrets inside customer clusters. The report, published 21 November 2026, does not say which credentials were compromised, how long the attackers had access, how many customers were affected, or what the secrets contained.

Why it matters

Kubernetes secrets often hold cloud API keys, database passwords, registry tokens and service principal credentials, including Entra ID app secrets that grant access to Microsoft 365 or Azure resources. Any credential stored in an affected Spot cluster should be treated as exposed, and attackers can reuse it well outside the Kubernetes environment. Exploitation is confirmed, but details are thin, so organizations using Rackspace Spot should act on the assumption of compromise rather than wait for more information.

What to do

  1. If you use Rackspace Spot, contact Rackspace to confirm whether your clusters were affected, and get the exposure window and the indicators they hold.
  2. Rotate every secret stored in Rackspace Spot clusters, starting with Entra ID app registration secrets and certificates, cloud provider keys, and database and registry credentials. Revoke the old values rather than only issuing new ones.
  3. Review Spot clusters for workloads, pods, service accounts or role bindings you did not create, and remove them.
  4. Review sign-in and activity logs for service principals and accounts whose credentials were held as Kubernetes secrets. Look for use from unfamiliar IP addresses or unusual API calls since the compromise window.
  5. Rotate the credentials used to access the Rackspace Spot console and API, and enforce MFA on those accounts.

Amazon says China-linked actor used AI agents to find and exploit a MONAI Label zero-day

MONAI Label · 1 source · first reported 21 November 2026

What happened

SecurityWeek, citing Amazon, reports that a China-linked threat actor used autonomous AI agents, which Amazon calls Cairn, to discover and exploit a critical zero-day in MONAI Label. MONAI Label is open-source medical imaging annotation server software. The available report does not give a CVE identifier, affected or fixed versions, technical details of the flaw, victims, or indicators of compromise.

Why it matters

Exploitation is confirmed in the wild, but MONAI Label is niche software that is mostly found in healthcare, research and AI/ML environments. Any instance exposed to the internet or reachable from untrusted networks should be treated as potentially compromised until a fix and details are published. The report that AI agents handled both discovery and exploitation suggests less-common research and ML tooling may be targeted faster than usual.

What to do

  1. Inventory all MONAI Label servers, including research workstations and GPU hosts, and remove any instance reachable from the internet or untrusted networks.
  2. Restrict access to MONAI Label servers to trusted networks behind VPN or authentication until the project or Amazon publishes fix details.
  3. Monitor Project MONAI and Amazon advisories for a CVE, affected versions and a patch, and apply the patch once it is released.
  4. Review MONAI Label hosts for unexpected child processes, new files, outbound connections and credential access, and rotate any cloud or dataset credentials stored on exposed hosts.
  5. Treat medical imaging data on exposed instances as possibly accessed, and involve privacy and compliance teams if compromise is suspected.

Entra Connect Health and the hybrid identity servers it watches

Entra Connect Health is Microsoft's cloud monitoring service for on-premises identity infrastructure, and it sits beside servers that attackers treat as keys to both Active Directory and Entra ID.

Why today. Microsoft has disclosed that attackers exploited a flaw in the Entra Connect Health service to read tenant administrator email addresses and tenant configuration. Knowing what the service touches, and what that leaked data is good for, tells you where to look next. See story 1.

What Connect Health is

Microsoft Entra Connect Health, formerly Azure AD Connect Health, monitors the on-premises side of a hybrid identity deployment. You install lightweight agents on Entra Connect Sync servers, AD FS servers and, optionally, domain controllers. The agents send health data, alerts, sync errors and, for AD FS, usage and sign-in analytics to a Microsoft-hosted service, and administrators view the results in the Entra admin center.

The service needs an Entra ID P1 or P2 licence. Registering an agent requires a privileged Entra role, and the agent then authenticates to the cloud service with its own credentials. The cloud side holds tenant-level metadata about your hybrid setup: which servers exist, how they are configured, which alerts fired, and who receives alert notifications.

Why the servers it monitors are tier zero

The Entra Connect Sync server holds credentials for an on-premises connector account. With password hash sync enabled, that account has directory replication permissions, the same rights a DCSync attack abuses. The server also holds credentials that let it write identities into Entra ID. Whoever controls the sync server can usually harvest on-premises password hashes and change what gets synchronized to the cloud.

AD FS servers hold the token-signing keys that Entra ID trusts for federated domains. An attacker with those keys can forge sign-in tokens. Both server types belong in the same protection tier as domain controllers, whether or not Connect Health is installed on them.

What leaked admin emails and tenant configuration give an attacker

Based on what has been reported so far, this breach was on Microsoft's service side and did not involve compromise of customer servers. The data still has value. A verified list of tenant administrator addresses is a ready-made target list for phishing, device code phishing, adversary-in-the-middle phishing and password spraying, because the attacker knows which accounts hold privilege.

Tenant configuration shows how identity is wired: whether the tenant is federated or uses password hash sync or pass-through authentication, which on-premises servers participate, and possibly their names and versions. That shortens reconnaissance for anyone planning to go after the sync or AD FS servers. Microsoft has not published the full scope, so treat both data types as exposed until it says otherwise.

What follow-on activity looks like in telemetry

Expect targeting of the privileged accounts first. In Sentinel or Defender XDR, look in SigninLogs and AADNonInteractiveUserSignInLogs for failed or unusual sign-ins against admin accounts, device code flow sign-ins, and new sign-in locations. Look in the email tables for lures sent to admin mailboxes.

For the hybrid servers, watch for directory replication requests from hosts that are not domain controllers; Defender for Identity raises alerts for this and its sensor can be installed on Entra Connect and AD FS servers. In AuditLogs, check for changes to federation settings, newly added federated domains, and new credentials or role assignments on accounts and applications used for directory synchronization.

For defenders

  1. Check the Microsoft 365 message center and Service Health for any notice about this incident addressed to your tenant, and record which admin addresses and configuration details Microsoft says were exposed.
  2. Require phishing-resistant MFA, such as FIDO2 keys or Windows Hello for Business, for every privileged Entra role, and block device code flow for admins through Conditional Access where you do not need it.
  3. Treat Entra Connect Sync and AD FS servers as tier zero: restrict interactive logon to tier-zero admins, install the Defender for Identity sensor on them, and confirm alerts for replication requests from non-domain-controller hosts are reaching your SOC.
  4. Hunt in AuditLogs for recent federation setting changes, new federated domains, and new credentials or role assignments on synchronization-related accounts and applications.
  5. Review which addresses receive Connect Health and other admin notifications, and use dedicated cloud-only admin accounts that are not used for everyday email.
