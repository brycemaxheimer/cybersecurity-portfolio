What happened

Check Point has released emergency hotfixes for CVE-2026-123039, a high-severity flaw that BleepingComputer reports attackers have exploited since late October 2026 to gain root and establish persistence on vulnerable devices. According to CISA, the Gaia OS DynamicCLI daemon runs as root and listens on a world-writable local socket without authenticating connecting processes. Any authenticated local user can send it commands that run as root. CISA lists Firewall, Security Gateway, Scalable Platforms, Security Management Server and Multi-Domain Security Management Server as affected, and added the CVE to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on 13 November 2026.

Why it matters

An attacker with any low-privilege foothold on a Check Point gateway or management server, such as stolen admin or service credentials or another bug, can take full root control and persist on a device that sits on the network perimeter. Exploitation is confirmed in the wild, public proof-of-concept code exists, and the CISA KEV due date is 16 November 2026, so this needs patching now despite the modest EPSS of 2.2% and CVSS of 7.8.

What to do

  1. Apply Check Point's emergency hotfix to every Gaia-based gateway, cluster member and management server before the CISA KEV due date of 16 November 2026.
  2. Treat devices exposed since late October as potentially compromised. Follow CISA forensics triage guidance and review them for unknown local accounts, modified startup scripts, cron jobs and unexpected root-owned binaries.
  3. Restrict SSH, Gaia Portal and management access to dedicated admin hosts, and audit all local and expert-mode accounts on Check Point devices.
  4. Rotate administrator, expert-mode and service credentials on Check Point devices after patching, especially on any device showing signs of tampering.
  5. Forward Gaia system and audit logs to Sentinel and alert on new account creation or privilege changes on gateways.

No. 02 · Priority 84

SCKit worm spreads through hijacked cloud-hosted ScreenConnect instances at MSPs

ConnectWise ScreenConnect · 2 sources · first reported 13 November 2026

What happened

ConnectWise reported from 11 November 2026 that a threat actor was gaining unauthorized access to cloud-hosted ScreenConnect instances belonging to managed service providers, and Microsoft Threat Intelligence has confirmed the activity. According to Microsoft, the attackers use ScreenConnect to silently install a second ScreenConnect client that reports to attacker infrastructure. They then deploy a worm called SCKit, which is unpacked by a batch launcher and a PowerShell decoder and persists through scheduled tasks and registry Run keys. Microsoft says a component named RelayTrap targets technicians who connect to infected devices, then abuses their authenticated sessions and management consoles to push payloads to other managed endpoints. Huntress separately reports unauthorized sessions disguised with cryptocurrency exchange names on more than 100 ScreenConnect servers, possibly as bait for technicians.

Why it matters

Exploitation is confirmed and widespread. Any organization whose endpoints are managed by an MSP through ScreenConnect may already host a rogue remote-access client. Because the worm moves from customer devices to technician workstations and back out through the MSP console, one compromised instance can reach many tenants. Microsoft warns that removing the rogue client alone does not remove SCKit's persistence or invalidate web sessions that were exposed.

What to do

  1. Ask your MSP whether its ScreenConnect instance was affected, and review the sessions in its console for unknown entries, including sessions named after cryptocurrency exchanges.
  2. Inventory every ScreenConnect client on your endpoints and remove any that connect to a host other than your approved MSP instance.
  3. On affected devices and technician workstations, remove the scheduled tasks and registry Run entries SCKit created rather than only uninstalling the rogue client.
  4. Invalidate ScreenConnect web sessions and rotate the credentials of technicians and administrators who connected to suspect devices.
  5. Restrict which ScreenConnect instances are allowed to run, for example through application control or outbound network allowlisting.
Affected
  • Cloud-hosted ConnectWise ScreenConnect instances operated by MSPs (no specific versions stated)

What happened

Why it matters

Both flaws are in KEV with a federal remediation due date of 16 November 2026, and public proof-of-concept code is reported for both (CVSS 8.6, EPSS 0.5% for UnrealIRCd; CVSS 7.8, EPSS 2.2% for Check Point). Because CISA has not said which Check Point products are affected, you will need to check the vendor advisory to scope your exposure.

What to do

  1. Find the Check Point advisory for CVE-2026-123039, work out which of your Check Point products and versions are affected, and apply the vendor fix or mitigation before 16 November 2026.
  2. Inventory any UnrealIRCd servers, especially linked multi-server networks, then apply the vendor mitigation or take them offline until patched.
  3. Because both flaws were exploited before disclosure, do a compromise assessment on affected Check Point and UnrealIRCd hosts, as CISA's forensics triage guidance expects, rather than only patching.
  4. Review administrative accounts, configuration changes and unexpected local permission changes on Check Point devices, and rotate credentials stored on any device that shows signs of tampering.
  5. Limit management-plane and IRC server-link access to trusted addresses only.

FBI warns Artex AI pentest framework is chaining intrusions to privileged access in under three hours

Artex · 2 sources · first reported 13 November 2026

What happened

According to The Record, the FBI issued a flash alert on 13 November 2026 warning that the open source offensive tool Artex is being used to automate intrusions, with some attacks moving from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours. Cisco Talos reports in-the-wild misuse of Artex and similar AI-enabled penetration testing frameworks such as Hephaestus, PentAGI and HexStrike AI. Talos says these frameworks automate reconnaissance, vulnerability testing and follow-on actions. They draw on open source tools like nmap and nuclei, internet mapping services, leaked-credential services, residential proxies and hash cracking services. The sources name no specific CVEs, and no indicators were included in the reporting available here.

Why it matters

Exploitation is confirmed in the wild, and the main risk is speed: internet-facing edge devices and exposed services with known vulnerabilities or reused credentials can be reached and escalated within hours, shrinking the window for patching and triage. Talos notes defenders may face these compromises without known indicators. Detection therefore depends on spotting automated scanning, credential attacks and rapid privilege escalation rather than matching IOCs.

What to do

  1. Inventory internet-facing edge devices and services and patch known exploited vulnerabilities on them first, since automated frameworks scan for these at scale.
  2. Enforce MFA and rotate any credentials that appear in leak data, prioritizing VPN, remote access and admin accounts.
  3. Alert on and rapidly triage bursts of inbound scanning followed by successful authentication or new privileged group membership, aiming for response times measured in minutes, not hours.
  4. Hunt for offensive tooling such as nmap, nuclei, hash crackers or AI agent frameworks running on internal hosts where they are not sanctioned.
  5. Review the FBI flash alert and Cisco Talos guidance for any indicators and add them to blocklists and watchlists once obtained.

Explainer

Background, not news

Unauthenticated local IPC: when a privileged daemon trusts any caller

A privileged service that accepts commands over a local socket or named pipe without checking who is connecting turns every local account into a root or SYSTEM account.

Why today. The Check Point Gaia flaw that CISA added to KEV is this exact pattern: a root-owned DynamicCLI daemon listening on a world-writable local socket that runs commands from any authenticated local user, exploited since late October 2026. See story 1, story 4.

How the weakness works

Many system services split work between an unprivileged front end, such as a CLI, web UI or agent, and a privileged back end that actually changes configuration. The two talk over local inter-process communication: Unix domain sockets on Linux-based appliances, and named pipes, local RPC or COM on Windows. The design is safe only if the privileged side decides who may connect and what each caller may ask for.

That decision can live in two places. The first is the endpoint's own access control: file permissions on a Unix socket path, or the security descriptor on a Windows named pipe. The second is a check inside the service, for example reading the connecting process's user ID through peer credentials on Linux, or identifying and impersonating the client on Windows before acting. When the endpoint is world-writable and the service does no caller check, the service becomes a confused deputy that executes whatever any local user sends it with its own privileges.

CISA's description of the Gaia bug, an incorrect permission assignment for a critical resource, is the standard weakness category for this: the code may be correct, but the permissions on the resource let the wrong people reach it.

What an attacker gains, especially on appliances

Local privilege escalation is often dismissed because it needs an existing foothold. On edge appliances that foothold is easier to get than it looks: a low-privilege admin or read-only account, a reused or phished credential, a web or VPN component that runs as a restricted user, or a separate bug that yields code execution as a service account. Escalating from there to root gives full control of the operating system beneath the vendor's management layer.

Root on a firewall or gateway means the attacker can install persistence that survives reboots and sometimes upgrades, read keys and credentials stored on the device, alter policy, and sit on the path that all traffic crosses. Appliances usually run no EDR, so activity at this level is largely invisible to the tools SOC teams rely on. That is why a high rather than critical severity rating on a local bug should not lower its priority when it is under active exploitation.

The same pattern on Windows

On Windows the equivalent is a service running as SYSTEM that exposes a named pipe, RPC interface or COM object with a permissive security descriptor and no check on the client. This class of bug appears regularly in third-party agents, including backup, monitoring, VPN and remote management software, because their installers create pipes for a user-facing tray app to talk to the privileged service.

Defenders can enumerate pipes and their permissions with tools such as Sysinternals AccessChk, and Microsoft Defender for Endpoint records named pipe activity as NamedPipeEvent entries in the DeviceEvents table. Sysmon can also log pipe creation and connection. Exact coverage and which pipes are recorded differ by sensor version and configuration, so verify what your tenant actually captures before relying on it for hunting.

What it looks like and how to prevent it

The telltale sign is a privileged service spawning processes or changing configuration on behalf of a user who should not be able to do so: a shell or script launched as root or SYSTEM whose parent is a management daemon, new accounts or scheduled jobs appearing without a matching admin change, or unexpected binaries in startup locations. On appliances this means forwarding the system and audit logs to your SIEM, because the device itself will not alert.

Prevention sits with both vendors and operators. Vendors should restrict socket and pipe permissions to the intended client and verify the caller inside the service. Operators should patch, keep local accounts on appliances to a minimum, and assume that a device exploited before patching may already carry persistence that the fix does not remove.

For defenders

  1. Apply Check Point's emergency hotfix for CVE-2026-123039 on every affected Gaia system, including management servers, and track it against the KEV due date.
  2. Because exploitation began in late October 2026, hunt affected Gaia devices for persistence installed before patching: unexpected local users, cron jobs, startup scripts and modified binaries, and rebuild from known-good images if anything is unexplained.
  3. Inventory every account that can log in to Gaia appliances, remove shared and unused ones, and restrict management access to dedicated admin networks so a stolen low-privilege credential cannot reach the device.
  4. Forward Gaia syslog and audit logs into Microsoft Sentinel and alert on root shells, new accounts and configuration changes that do not match a change ticket.
  5. On Windows fleets, use AccessChk or Defender for Endpoint NamedPipeEvent data to find named pipes owned by SYSTEM services that grant write access to Everyone or Users, and raise any you find with the software vendor.
