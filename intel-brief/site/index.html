Ivanti patches two more Connect Secure zero-days chained with CVE-2026-104935; CISA adds both to KEV

Ivanti Connect Secure · 3 sources · first reported 2 November 2026

Also: CVE-2026-111357 (KEV), CVE-2026-118527 (KEV)

What happened

On 29 October 2026, Ivanti updated its Connect Secure advisory with fixes for two more vulnerabilities, according to Rapid7. CVE-2026-111357 is a local authenticated privilege escalation, and CVE-2026-118527 is an authenticated file upload to a predictable path. Ivanti says both were exploited before disclosure against a limited number of customers, chained with the authentication bypass CVE-2026-104935. CISA added CVE-2026-111357 and CVE-2026-118527 to KEV on 30 October 2026. Volexity attributes the activity to UTA0576, which gained unauthenticated root on an appliance running 22.8R2.4 starting 3 October 2026; BleepingComputer dates the attacks to at least 1 October.

Why it matters

The chain gives an unauthenticated attacker root on an internet-facing VPN gateway. Volexity saw follow-on credential theft and Active Directory queries for Windows LAPS passwords and BitLocker recovery keys, so a compromised appliance puts the domain at risk. All three CVEs are in KEV, and CVE-2026-104935 has public proof-of-concept code and an EPSS of 86.8%, so any appliance still unpatched should be treated as possibly compromised.

What to do

  1. Apply Ivanti's updated Connect Secure fixes for CVE-2026-104935, CVE-2026-111357 and CVE-2026-118527 immediately, using the fixed builds listed in Ivanti's advisory.
  2. Run Ivanti's integrity checking tool on every Connect Secure appliance and review appliance logs back to at least 1 October 2026 for signs of compromise.
  3. If compromise is suspected, rotate credentials that passed through or are stored on the appliance, including service accounts used for LDAP or AD binding.
  4. Rotate Windows LAPS passwords and BitLocker recovery keys that the appliance's AD account could read, and review directory read auditing for bulk access to those attributes.
  5. Restrict the appliance's AD service account to the least privilege it needs, and limit its network access to domain controllers.

Over 1,000 internet-exposed GitLab servers remain unpatched against an actively exploited RCE flaw

GitLab GitLab · 1 source · first reported 2 November 2026

What happened

BleepingComputer reported on 2 November 2026 that more than 1,000 internet-facing GitLab servers are still unpatched against a maximum-severity vulnerability that attackers are exploiting for remote code execution. The available reporting does not name the CVE, the affected or fixed GitLab versions, the attackers, or how the exposure count was measured. GitLab's own advisory was not among the sources, so those details still need checking with the vendor.

Why it matters

Self-managed GitLab instances hold source code, CI/CD secrets, deploy keys and runner tokens. A compromised server can therefore lead to supply-chain tampering and lateral movement into cloud and on-premises environments. Exploitation is reported as ongoing, so any internet-exposed self-managed GitLab instance that has not been patched should be treated as potentially compromised.

What to do

  1. Inventory all self-managed GitLab instances, especially internet-facing ones, and check GitLab's security release notes to find the vulnerability and fixed version that BleepingComputer refers to.
  2. Upgrade exposed instances to the latest GitLab security release immediately. If you cannot patch right away, remove them from direct internet exposure or restrict access with a VPN or IP allowlist.
  3. On any instance that was exposed while unpatched, look for compromise: unexpected shell or download-tool processes spawned by GitLab services, new files in web-served directories, and new admin users or personal access tokens.
  4. If compromise is suspected, rotate CI/CD variables, runner registration tokens, deploy keys, personal access tokens and any cloud credentials stored in GitLab.
  5. Review recent commits, pipeline definitions and protected-branch changes for unauthorized modifications.

What happened

The vulnerable parameters sit in the web management interface, the OpenVPN and WireGuard group handlers, and DNS resolution. The sixth, CVE-2026-120281, is a stack-based buffer overflow in the OpenVPN handler. NVD says all six can be triggered remotely and that exploit details have been made public, though public proof-of-concept tracking does not yet confirm working code, and NVD lists no vendor fix.

Why it matters

These are SOHO-class routers, but they often turn up at branch offices and in home-worker setups that carry corporate VPN traffic, and command injection usually means full device takeover. Five of the CVEs are rated CVSS 9.3 and one 8.6 (EPSS 1.7%). None is in CISA KEV and no in-the-wild exploitation is reported, but publicly disclosed exploit details raise the risk for any management interface reachable from untrusted networks.

What to do

  1. Inventory WAVLINK WN553X1-B, WN536AX6-A and WN551X3-A devices on corporate, branch and remote-worker networks.
  2. Block internet and guest-network access to the router web management interface and restrict it to a trusted management VLAN.
  3. Check WAVLINK for updated firmware and apply it once released; replace end-of-support or unpatched units used for business traffic with managed edge devices.
  4. Disable unused OpenVPN and WireGuard features on affected routers to reduce exposed handlers.
  5. Treat affected routers as untrusted: route corporate traffic through an enforced VPN and watch for unexpected configuration or DNS changes.

Background, not news

Exploit chaining on edge appliances, and why patching does not evict an attacker

Exploit chaining links several individually limited flaws, such as an authentication bypass, a privilege escalation and a file write, into one path from the internet to full control of a device.

Why today. Ivanti's two new Connect Secure fixes close links in a chain that attackers had already used with an authentication bypass to get unauthenticated root on VPN gateways, so patched appliances may still hold an attacker's foothold. The same reasoning applies to the actively exploited GitLab servers that remain exposed. See story 1, story 3, story 8.

How a chain works

Most single vulnerabilities give an attacker only one capability. An authentication bypass reaches functions meant for logged-in users. A privilege escalation turns a low-privileged foothold into root. A file upload to a predictable path places attacker content where the system will later read or run it. On its own, each flaw may score only moderately in CVSS.

Chained, the output of one step becomes the input to the next. The bypass supplies the authenticated session that the upload flaw requires, and the escalation turns the resulting code into root. This is why Ivanti's newly fixed upload flaw, rated low as a standalone issue, still landed in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog: its rating describes it in isolation, not its role in the chain.

For defenders, two things follow. Severity scores understate the risk of authenticated or local flaws on internet-facing devices, because attackers often already hold the bypass. And when a vendor adds flaws to an existing advisory, the earlier patch did not close every link.

Why edge devices are the favourite target

VPN gateways, firewalls and code platforms such as GitLab sit on the internet, hold credentials, and bridge into internal networks. Appliances are closed systems. You usually cannot run an EDR agent such as Defender for Endpoint on them, and their logs are often thin, kept only locally, or overwritten.

Root on a VPN appliance can give an attacker the ability to capture user credentials and session cookies, read the stored directory binding account, change configuration, and plant persistence that survives reboots and sometimes upgrades. The appliance's internal IP address then becomes a trusted launch point into the rest of the network.

Why patching is not remediation

A patch removes the vulnerable code path but does nothing to a webshell, implant, modified file or stolen credential already in place. If exploitation began before you patched, and for zero-days it did by definition, you need a compromise assessment as well as the update.

Vendors differ in what they provide. Ivanti publishes an Integrity Checker Tool for Connect Secure, with internal and external variants. In previous Ivanti incidents, though, attackers tried to tamper with or evade the checks, so a clean result does not prove the device is clean. For appliances where compromise is suspected, CISA and vendors have repeatedly advised a factory reset or rebuild before applying the fixed version. Follow the current vendor advisory for the exact procedure for your version.

What it looks like in your telemetry

Because the appliance itself is hard to see into, hunt around it. In Sentinel, look at forwarded syslog from the appliance for unexpected administrative logins, configuration changes, reboots and gaps in logging. Look at web or proxy logs for unusual requests to management or upload paths.

In Defender XDR and Entra ID, treat the appliance's internal address as a suspect source. Watch for LDAP or Kerberos activity from the directory binding account outside its normal pattern, RDP, SMB or WinRM sessions originating from the VPN's address, and sign-ins by VPN users from unfamiliar locations shortly after their VPN session, which can indicate stolen session material.

For defenders

  1. Confirm every Ivanti Connect Secure appliance runs the version fixed in the updated 29 October 2026 advisory, and treat any appliance that was internet-exposed during October as possibly compromised rather than simply unpatched.
  2. Run the vendor integrity check externally as well as on the device, and plan a factory reset and rebuild for any appliance with suspicious results or log gaps, then reapply the fixed image.
  3. After a suspected compromise, rotate the appliance's directory binding and service account credentials, local admin passwords and certificates, and revoke active VPN user sessions.
  4. Forward appliance syslog to Sentinel with retention long enough to cover a zero-day window, and alert on admin logins, configuration changes and logging interruptions.
  5. Build hunts in Defender XDR for lateral movement and unusual authentication originating from VPN and GitLab server IP addresses, and include authenticated or local flaws on edge devices in your emergency patch scope regardless of CVSS score.
