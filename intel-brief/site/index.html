What happened

Ivanti updated its Connect Secure advisory on 29 October 2026 to add CVE-2026-111357 (local authenticated privilege escalation) and CVE-2026-118527 (authenticated file upload to a predictable disk path), and released fixes for both, according to Rapid7. Ivanti says a limited number of customers were exploited before disclosure through a chain combining these with CVE-2026-104935, an authentication bypass leading to remote unauthenticated command execution that was patched around 13 October 2026. Volexity attributes the activity to UTA0576, which gained unauthenticated root access to an appliance running 22.8R2.4 starting 3 October 2026, then stole credentials and queried Active Directory for Windows LAPS passwords and BitLocker recovery keys. CISA added CVE-2026-111357 and CVE-2026-118527 to KEV on 30 October 2026; CVE-2026-104935 is also listed.

Why it matters

A compromised VPN appliance gives the attacker root on the edge and a foothold to harvest domain credentials, local admin passwords and disk recovery keys, so the impact reaches far into Windows and AD. All three CVEs are in KEV with confirmed in-the-wild exploitation, CVE-2026-104935 has public proof-of-concept code and an EPSS of 88.9%, and the sources give no KEV due date. Scores differ for CVE-2026-111357 (Ivanti rates it 8.8, the CVE record 7.0), but the chained impact warrants emergency remediation regardless.

What to do

  1. Apply Ivanti's latest Connect Secure fixes covering CVE-2026-104935, CVE-2026-111357 and CVE-2026-118527 immediately; the sources do not list fixed build numbers, so confirm them in Ivanti's advisory.
  2. Treat any appliance unpatched before 29 October 2026 as potentially compromised: run Ivanti's integrity checking, preserve logs, and rebuild from a known-good image if anything is suspicious.
  3. Rotate credentials that transited or were stored on the appliance, including service accounts used for LDAP/AD binding and VPN user passwords, and reset sessions.
  4. Rotate Windows LAPS passwords and BitLocker recovery keys that may have been read from AD since 3 October 2026, prioritizing any queried from the appliance's IP or its service account.
  5. Restrict the appliance's LDAP access to the minimum attributes it needs and remove read rights to LAPS and BitLocker recovery attributes from its service account.

Microsoft details fake-download campaign pushing trojanized Inno Setup installers with monetization payloads

Microsoft · 1 source · first reported 3 November 2026

What happened

Microsoft Threat Intelligence reported on 3 November 2026 an ongoing fake-download campaign that targets people searching for cracked software, free games, activators, plugins and unofficial utilities. According to Microsoft, trojanized Inno Setup-style installers stage a temporary runner under the user's temp folder, which launches numbered stages and calls back to rotating infrastructure to deliver several monetized payloads, called offers, in one session. Microsoft confirmed three monetization outcomes: turning the device into a proxy for third-party internet traffic, enrolling it in a distributed-computing platform, and silently installing extra utility-style software. The source text available here is truncated, so the full set of indicators and Microsoft's hunting queries is not included.

Why it matters

Proxyware on a corporate endpoint routes unknown third-party traffic through your egress IP, which can tie your organization to abuse and adds unmanaged software and persistence to the device. Microsoft describes the activity as active and evolving, and it relies on users installing software from untrusted sources, so any fleet without application control or PUA blocking is exposed. Treat a hit as a policy violation and a possible foothold, not just adware.

What to do

  1. Enable potentially unwanted application (PUA) protection in Microsoft Defender Antivirus in block mode across all endpoints.
  2. Restrict software installation to trusted sources using application control (WDAC or AppLocker) and remove local admin rights where possible.
  3. Hunt for Inno Setup temporary runners under the temp folder launching eld*.exe or set_*.exe stages, and for callbacks containing the offer_execution pattern.
  4. On affected devices, inventory and remove newly installed proxyware, distributed-computing clients and bundled utilities, along with their Run keys, services and scheduled tasks.
  5. Review egress traffic from affected hosts for unexpected proxy or relay connections and block the campaign infrastructure once Microsoft's published indicators are obtained.

No. 03 · Priority 57

Google and partners take down XCPE botnet of 1.3 million Android TV boxes, cut ties with Videostrong

Videostrong Technology XCPE botnet · 3 sources · first reported 2 November 2026

What happened

Google and industry partners have dismantled the XCPE botnet, which Google says included more than 1.3 million Android TV set-top boxes that their manufacturer, China-based Videostrong Technology, deliberately shipped with malware. According to the Google Threat Intelligence Group, as reported by Krebs on Security, the malware sold the devices' internet connections to residential proxy services, committed ad fraud, and let Videostrong sell backdoor access to criminal affiliates who installed their own malware modules. Google says it took legal action to seize the domains and servers used for control and malware delivery, and it has ended its relationship with Videostrong. The same malware was also found in other Android-based IoT products, including telehealth devices, karaoke machines and digital signage. Experts quoted by Krebs say the takedown may also cut the botnet off from more than 10 million additional compromised systems.

Why it matters

Cheap Android TV boxes and signage players often end up on corporate, guest or retail networks without anyone managing them. An infected box can serve as a residential proxy exit point or an entry point for affiliate malware inside your perimeter. Separately, residential proxy networks like this one are a major source of the account-takeover and password-spraying traffic that hits Entra ID, because the logins appear to come from ordinary consumer IP addresses.

What to do

  1. Inventory Android TV boxes, digital signage players and other uncertified Android IoT devices on corporate and guest networks. Remove or replace any made by Videostrong or of unknown origin.
  2. Move any IoT devices you must keep onto isolated VLANs with egress filtering, so they cannot reach internal systems or act as unmonitored proxies.
  3. Use Defender for Endpoint device discovery to find unmanaged Android and smart-TV class devices, and review their outbound traffic at the firewall.
  4. Watch Entra ID sign-ins for password spraying and account takeover from residential and consumer ISP address space. Enforce phishing-resistant MFA and Conditional Access location and device controls.
  5. Look for Google Threat Intelligence Group indicators when they are published, and add them to firewall and DNS blocklists.
Affected
  • Android TV set-top boxes and other Android-based IoT devices made by Videostrong Technology (specific models not stated in the sources)

What happened

What to do

  1. Find every refrigeration, HVAC and building-management controller reachable from the internet, including those run by contractors, and remove direct exposure or place it behind a VPN with MFA.
  2. Change default and shared credentials on controller web interfaces and on remote-maintenance accounts, and confirm which vendors and contractors hold remote access.
  3. Restrict inbound access to OT management interfaces at the firewall to known contractor IP ranges, and log all access attempts.
  4. Turn on temperature and setpoint-change alarms that notify on-site staff, so tampering is caught before stock spoils.
  5. Track vendor and national CERT advisories for the affected controller product as details are released.

3AM ransomware affiliate encrypts about 37 machines at UK school estate from an unmanaged host

1 source · first reported 3 November 2026

What happened

BleepingComputer reports that a 3AM ransomware affiliate attacked a school estate in the UK and encrypted roughly 37 of the nearly 50 machines it reached. According to the report, the encryption was launched from an unmanaged host rather than from endpoints running security tooling. BleepingComputer's headline describes the attack as suspected AI-assisted, but the available text does not explain that claim, the initial access vector or the tools used.

Why it matters

Encrypting over the network from a device with no EDR agent bypasses endpoint ransomware protections on the victim machines. File shares and servers can then be encrypted with no malicious process ever running on them. Exploitation is confirmed, but the sources give no CVEs, indicators or initial access details, so defenders should focus on coverage gaps and on detecting mass remote file modification over SMB.

What to do

  1. Inventory unmanaged and non-onboarded devices on internal networks and onboard them to Defender for Endpoint or isolate them from server and workstation segments.
  2. Restrict inbound SMB (TCP 445) between workstations and limit SMB access to file servers to known management and client ranges.
  3. Enable Defender for Endpoint attack disruption, tamper protection and controlled folder access so remote encryption can be blocked at the target and the source can be contained.
  4. Hunt for single source IPs or accounts creating, modifying or renaming large numbers of files over SMB across multiple devices in a short window.
  5. Verify that offline or immutable backups exist for school and other critical estates and test restoration.

Explainer

Background, not news

LAPS passwords and BitLocker recovery keys as Active Directory secrets

Active Directory can hold local administrator passwords and BitLocker recovery keys for every domain-joined computer, so any account allowed to read them becomes a skeleton key to your endpoints.

Why today. Volexity says UTA0576 used its root access on an Ivanti Connect Secure appliance to steal credentials and then query Active Directory for Windows LAPS passwords and BitLocker recovery keys. That is how one edge-device compromise turns into local admin and disk access across the Windows estate. See story 1.

Where the secrets live

Legacy Microsoft LAPS writes each computer's local administrator password in clear text to the ms-Mcs-AdmPwd attribute on the computer object, with the expiry in ms-Mcs-AdmPwdExpirationTime. Windows LAPS, the version now built into supported Windows releases, can back up to Active Directory or to Entra ID. In Active Directory it uses msLAPS-Password for clear text, or msLAPS-EncryptedPassword when encryption is enabled, which requires a domain functional level of 2016 or later. It can also store the domain controller DSRM password.

BitLocker recovery information, when escrowed to Active Directory, is stored as msFVE-RecoveryInformation child objects under each computer object. The 48-digit recovery password sits in the msFVE-RecoveryPassword attribute. A recovery key unlocks the drive without the TPM or PIN, so whoever holds it can read the disk offline or get past pre-boot protection.

Who can read them

These attributes are marked confidential. A normal authenticated user cannot read them, but Domain Admins can, and so can any account or group that has been granted Control Access on the attribute. The All Extended Rights permission also grants that read, and it is often handed out broadly through OU delegation, helpdesk groups or service accounts. Windows LAPS encryption adds a second check: only the principal named as the authorized decryptor (Domain Admins by default) can decrypt the password, even if other accounts can read the attribute.

None of this helps once an attacker holds credentials for an authorized account. VPN appliances often store an LDAP bind account or cache administrators' logons, so a rooted gateway can supply exactly the identity needed. With LAPS passwords the attacker gets local admin on each machine, which supports lateral movement and credential dumping. With recovery keys they get access to the data on stolen or imaged disks.

What it looks like in telemetry

Reads of these attributes leave no trace by default. On domain controllers, enable Audit Directory Service Access and add a SACL entry that audits read-property on the LAPS attributes and on msFVE-RecoveryInformation objects. Reads then appear as event 4662, which you can query in Sentinel's SecurityEvent table, filtered to the attribute or object schema GUIDs. With Defender for Identity deployed, the IdentityQueryEvents table in Defender XDR records LDAP queries against domain controllers. Bulk queries for these attributes, especially from an appliance's service account or from an unusual source IP, deserve an alert.

Watch for one account reading many computers' secrets in a short time, and for reads that come from a VPN appliance's address range or bind account. Also watch for interactive or LDAP activity by admin accounts at hours that don't fit their normal pattern. The Windows LAPS operational log on each client records password rotation, not reads, so detection of reads has to happen on the domain controllers.

Reducing the blast radius

Minimize who can read these attributes and make sure none of those accounts can authenticate from, or is stored on, an edge device. Inventory read rights with Find-LapsADExtendedRights for Windows LAPS or Find-AdmPwdExtendedRights for legacy LAPS, and review BitLocker recovery delegation the same way. Where Windows LAPS is used, enable password encryption and restrict the decryptor group. If you suspect exposure, rotate every LAPS password by forcing expiry or with Reset-LapsPassword, and rotate BitLocker recovery passwords. Treating these values as already stolen is safer than trusting the audit trail.

For defenders

  1. If you run Ivanti Connect Secure and were exposed during the exploitation window, force rotation of all LAPS passwords and BitLocker recovery passwords for domains the appliance could reach, and reset the appliance's LDAP bind account and any admin credentials used on it.
  2. Run Find-LapsADExtendedRights or Find-AdmPwdExtendedRights on every OU, and remove All Extended Rights and Control Access grants from service accounts, helpdesk groups and anything an edge device authenticates as.
  3. Add SACL auditing for reads of the LAPS password attributes and msFVE-RecoveryInformation objects on domain controllers, then build a Sentinel rule on event 4662 that alerts when one account reads secrets for many computers.
  4. In Defender XDR, hunt IdentityQueryEvents for LDAP queries naming LAPS or BitLocker recovery attributes that come from VPN appliance IPs or bind accounts.
  5. Move legacy LAPS to Windows LAPS with password encryption enabled and a tightly scoped decryptor group, or back up to Entra ID where that fits your management model.
