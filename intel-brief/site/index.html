Microsoft warns of high-volume phishing using fake Microsoft 365 service-health outage notices

Microsoft Microsoft 365 · 1 source · first reported 17 November 2026

What happened

Microsoft Threat Intelligence says it observed a coordinated, high-volume phishing campaign on 13 November 2026 that targeted organizations across multiple sectors worldwide with emails posing as Microsoft 365 service-health notifications. The lures claimed messages had been held during an Exchange Online disruption and would be deleted within 24 hours unless the recipient clicked a link. According to Microsoft, the emails used subjects of the form "Outage – <random token>", display names made of a three-letter prefix plus "Outage Notification" or "Service Health", recipient-specific tracking links and a common non-Microsoft destination. Microsoft assesses the activity was likely credential phishing but states it has not confirmed credential capture, malware delivery or post-compromise activity.

Why it matters

Any Microsoft 365 tenant is a plausible target, and randomized subjects and per-recipient links are designed to defeat simple pattern blocking. If users entered credentials, Microsoft flags risks of mailbox access, business email compromise, internal phishing and inbox-rule manipulation, so clicks since 13 November 2026 should be triaged now. Microsoft says Defender for Office 365 blocks and quarantines these messages, but delivered copies and clicks before blocking still need review.

What to do

  1. Search mail telemetry for messages since 13 November 2026 with subjects starting "Outage –" and sender display names ending in "Outage Notification" or "Service Health", and purge any delivered copies.
  2. Identify users who clicked links in those messages, reset their passwords, revoke sessions and refresh tokens, and review their sign-in logs for unfamiliar IPs or devices.
  3. Review mailbox rules and forwarding settings created after the click for affected users, removing rules that delete, move or forward mail.
  4. Warn users that Microsoft does not send held-mail deletion notices of this kind and that real service-health status is shown in the admin center.
  5. Enforce phishing-resistant MFA and Conditional Access for users exposed to the campaign.

What happened

Huntress reports confirmed exploitation of CVE-2026-84869 in ConnectWise ScreenConnect, with attackers using it to run malicious files through the accounts of managed service provider technicians. The flaw is rated CVSS 9.9 (critical) and is listed in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. The sources reviewed do not give affected or fixed version numbers, technical root cause, or the specific indicators Huntress published.

Why it matters

ScreenConnect gives remote, high-privilege access to every endpoint it manages. A compromised MSP technician session can push and run files across many customer Windows servers and workstations at once. Exploitation is confirmed and the CVE is in CISA KEV, although no KEV due date is listed. EPSS is 1.2% and no public proof-of-concept is reported, but active exploitation should take priority over those scores.

What to do

  1. Identify every ScreenConnect server and client in your estate and at your MSPs, then apply the ConnectWise fix for CVE-2026-84869 as soon as ConnectWise confirms the patched version.
  2. Ask your MSP whether its ScreenConnect instance is patched and whether it has reviewed technician accounts and sessions for misuse.
  3. Hunt for files dropped and processes launched by ScreenConnect client services, especially script interpreters and newly written executables.
  4. Review ScreenConnect technician accounts, enforce MFA, and rotate credentials for any account showing unexpected sessions or file transfers.
  5. Collect the attack-chain indicators Huntress published and sweep endpoint and network telemetry for them.

Barracuda attributes November cloud breach to stolen cloud access key and ARTEX AI pentest framework

Barracuda · 1 source · first reported 16 November 2026

What happened

BleepingComputer reported on 16 November 2026 that Barracuda has linked a breach of its cloud environment, which happened earlier in November, to attackers using a stolen cloud access key. According to the report, the intruders also used ARTEX, an AI-powered penetration testing framework. The available reporting does not say which Barracuda products or customer data were affected, how the key was stolen, or what the attackers did once inside.

Why it matters

Barracuda sells email and network security products, and many of them connect to Microsoft 365 tenants through app consents, API tokens or connector credentials. Any customer data or integration secrets held in Barracuda's cloud could be misused. The sources do not confirm customer impact, so treat this as a supply-chain exposure check until Barracuda publishes details, not as a confirmed compromise of your tenant.

What to do

  1. Watch for Barracuda customer notifications and advisories about this incident, and follow any credential rotation guidance they issue.
  2. Inventory Barracuda integrations with Microsoft 365 and Entra ID, including enterprise apps, service principals, API keys and mail connectors, and confirm each one is still needed.
  3. As a precaution, rotate secrets and API tokens shared with Barracuda cloud services, and review the permissions granted to Barracuda apps in Entra ID.
  4. Review recent activity by Barracuda-related apps and service principals in M365 audit logs and look for unusual mailbox access, configuration changes or new connectors.
  5. Audit your own cloud access keys for long-lived or overprivileged credentials, since a stolen key was the reported entry point.

What happened

Rapid7 reports that CVE-2026-90436, an unauthenticated remote code execution flaw caused by unsafe reflection in Apache Axis 1.x, was disclosed by the Apache Software Foundation on 6 November 2026. Some versions of Atlassian Confluence Data Center and N-able N-central bundle the vulnerable library and are exploitable when running a non-default configuration. Rapid7 MDR saw exploitation against a Confluence server on 13 November 2026. On the same day N-able reported in-the-wild exploitation of N-central for a flaw that Rapid7 says strongly correlates to this CVE, and a public proof of concept targeting N-central had been released on 12 November 2026.

Why it matters

Confluence and N-central are often reachable from the internet, and N-central has privileged reach into every managed endpoint, so a compromise can spread quickly into Windows estates. Exploitation is confirmed and public exploit code exists, but the CVE is not yet in CISA KEV and EPSS is only 1.1%, so do not let those scores drive prioritization. Rapid7 observed attackers dumping Confluence database contents within about three minutes of first execution and recommends emergency remediation outside normal patch cycles.

What to do

  1. Inventory internet-exposed Confluence Data Center and N-central instances and apply the vendor fixes on an emergency basis. Check the Atlassian and N-able advisories for fixed versions, because the sources do not list them.
  2. Until patched, block external access to Axis SOAP endpoints such as /plugins/servlet/soap-axis1/ on Confluence and restrict N-central management interfaces to trusted networks.
  3. Hunt for the Confluence or N-central Java process spawning shells, curl or wget, and for outbound connections to Pinggy or Cloudflare Tunnel hosts.
  4. Review Confluence access logs for unauthenticated POST requests to the soap-axis1 servlet, including requests that returned HTTP 500.
  5. If you find signs of compromise, rotate Confluence database credentials and encryption keys and any credentials stored in or reachable from N-central, then review MySQL access from the application host.

What happened

SANS ISC reports that its honeypots began receiving POST requests to the LiteLLM endpoint /mcp-rest/test/tools/list over the weekend of 14-15 November 2026, in what it assesses as attempts to exploit CVE-2026-42271 (LiteLLM) and CVE-2026-48710 (Starlette). According to SANS ISC, Starlette versions before 1.0.1 do not validate the Host header (dubbed BadHost) and use it to build the URL that drives access control decisions, which lets attackers reach the normally authenticated LiteLLM endpoint. SANS ISC cites Horizon3.ai research showing the two flaws can be chained for unauthenticated remote code execution. The observed requests used malformed Host values such as 'localhost]', '[localhost', '127.0.0.1]' and 'litellm.proxy', apparently to evade filters built around the public proof of concept.

Why it matters

Both CVEs are in CISA KEV and public proof-of-concept code exists. EPSS is 91.4% for CVE-2026-42271 and 70.3% for CVE-2026-48710, and SANS ISC notes exploitation was first reported in July 2026, so this is renewed activity rather than a new bug. Any internet-reachable LiteLLM proxy or Starlette-based AI gateway, including Red Hat OpenShift AI and AI Inference Server deployments, should be treated as a likely target, and successful exploitation runs commands as the LiteLLM service account.

What to do

  1. Inventory LiteLLM and Starlette deployments and upgrade Starlette to 1.0.1 or later; apply the vendor-fixed LiteLLM release and the Red Hat updates for affected products.
  2. Remove LiteLLM proxies and admin or MCP endpoints from direct internet exposure, or put them behind authenticated access.
  3. At the reverse proxy or WAF, allow only expected hostnames in the Host header and reject Host values containing characters like '[', ']', '/', '?' or '#'. Do not match on the specific exploit strings.
  4. Search web and proxy logs for POST requests to /mcp-rest/test/tools/list and for unusual Host headers. Investigate any LiteLLM host where the Python process spawned shells or reconnaissance commands.
  5. On any host showing compromise, rotate the LLM provider API keys and the other secrets stored in the LiteLLM configuration.

  • Starlette versions prior to 1.0.1 (CVE-2026-48710)
  • LiteLLM (CVE-2026-42271); affected version range not stated in the sources
  • Red Hat OpenShift AI, AI Inference Server, Ansible Automation Platform and Migration Toolkit for Applications are listed as affected products; versions not stated

Explainer

Background, not news

HTTP/3 and QUIC as a command-and-control channel

QUIC is an encrypted transport that runs over UDP and carries HTTP/3; malware that uses it for command and control can slip past network controls built to inspect TCP-based HTTPS.

Why today. Unit 42 reports that two implants used against Southeast Asian governments, PerfectPioneer RAT and KUKURAT, communicate over HTTP/3. Defenders should understand why that choice can reduce visibility at the proxy and firewall, and what telemetry still exposes it. See story 6.

What QUIC and HTTP/3 are

QUIC is a transport protocol standardized by the IETF in RFC 9000, and HTTP/3 (RFC 9114) is the version of HTTP that runs on top of it. Classic HTTPS uses TCP plus a separate TLS layer. QUIC instead runs over UDP, usually on port 443, and builds TLS 1.3 directly into the transport.

Major browsers and large services such as Google, Microsoft, Cloudflare and Meta use QUIC widely, so UDP 443 traffic is normal on most networks. Windows also ships its own QUIC implementation, MsQuic, which features such as SMB over QUIC use. Malware does not need an operating system component, though. An implant can bundle an open-source QUIC library and speak HTTP/3 to its server on its own.

Why attackers choose it for C2

Much more of a QUIC connection is encrypted than with TLS over TCP. Packet headers are largely protected, and only the initial handshake packets can be decrypted by an observer who knows the protocol. Many firewalls, secure web gateways and TLS-inspection proxies were built around TCP. Depending on vendor and version, they may not decrypt QUIC at all, may only read the server name from the handshake, or may let it pass as generic UDP.

QUIC also supports connection migration: a session is tied to connection IDs rather than to a fixed IP address and port, so it can survive network changes. That helps a long-lived implant stay connected, but it also makes simple flow-based tracking less reliable. The net effect is C2 that blends with legitimate browser traffic while avoiding the content inspection and URL categorization the organization relies on for HTTPS.

What it looks like in telemetry

Even when the payload is opaque, the endpoint still sees which process opened the socket. On Windows, the most reliable signal is a process that is not a browser or known QUIC client making outbound UDP connections to port 443 on external addresses. Defender for Endpoint network events include protocol, remote IP, port and the initiating process, so check what your sensor records for UDP in your environment.

At the network layer, firewall or NetFlow logs showing sustained or periodic UDP 443 sessions from servers or workstations to low-reputation or recently registered infrastructure are worth reviewing. So are QUIC handshakes whose server name does not match a known service. Beaconing analysis works on QUIC flows just as it does on TCP: look for regular intervals, consistent sizes and a single destination contacted by few hosts.

How to reduce the risk

The common vendor recommendation is to block outbound UDP 443 at the perimeter, except to destinations that genuinely need it. Browsers then fall back to TCP-based HTTPS, which your proxy can inspect. Chrome and Edge also offer an enterprise policy to disable QUIC, which keeps browser traffic consistent with that rule. If you must allow QUIC, confirm what your firewall or gateway actually inspects for it and treat uninspected UDP 443 as a gap to monitor rather than as trusted web traffic.

For defenders

  1. Find out whether your firewall or secure web gateway decrypts, partially inspects, or simply passes QUIC on UDP 443, and record the answer for your current version.
  2. Block outbound UDP 443 by default and allow it only to required destinations, so browsers fall back to inspectable TCP HTTPS; set the Chrome and Edge policies that disable QUIC to match.
  3. Hunt in Defender XDR for outbound UDP connections to remote port 443 from processes other than approved browsers and known QUIC clients, prioritizing servers and unsigned or user-writable binaries.
  4. Add UDP 443 flows to beaconing analysis in Sentinel, looking for regular intervals to rare external destinations contacted by only a few hosts.
  5. Check whether servers that do not use SMB over QUIC or other QUIC services can reach the internet over UDP at all, and restrict them if not needed.
